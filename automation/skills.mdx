A **Skill** is a saved process that tells Ari how to handle a recurring situation: what steps to take, what conventions to follow, and when to activate at all. Once saved, Ari activates a Skill on its own when a request matches its trigger condition — you don't need to reference it by name.

## Built-in and saved Skills

cfo.ai ships with built-in Skills covering common workflows. Your workspace can also hold its own saved Skills, written for how your team specifically works. Ask Ari to list what's available, including a search by name, to see both together.

## Create a Skill

Ask Ari to save a new Skill and give it:

- **A name** — lowercase letters, digits, and hyphens, like `board-report`.
- **A trigger condition** — phrased as when it should activate, for example "Use when the user asks for a board or investor report."
- **Instructions** — the full workflow: the steps, structure, and conventions Ari should follow once the Skill activates.

A good trigger condition is specific enough that Ari doesn't activate the Skill for unrelated requests, but general enough to catch the different ways someone might phrase the same ask.

## Update or remove a Skill

Ask Ari to update a saved Skill's description or instructions, or to delete one you no longer need. Built-in Skills can't be edited or deleted — if a built-in Skill needs to work differently for your workspace, ask Ari to save a new one with a different name instead.

## Sample custom Skill prompts

These are complete, ready-to-save Skills. Each row gives the three things Ari needs: a name, a trigger condition, and the instructions to follow once the Skill activates. Copy a row and ask Ari to save it, or adapt the instructions to how your team works.

| Skill name | Description | Instructions for Ari |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `driver-model-scaffold` | Use when building a driver-based model from scratch, adding a variable that rolls forward across periods, or debugging a model where drill-in rows read zero, a formula reports a circular reference, or the first period looks inflated. | Build one layer at a time and verify each before adding the next: the volume or capacity spine the business scales on, then per-unit economics, then revenue, then costs, then headcount, then the P&L. Read every table back and confirm it has no formula errors before continuing. When a variable is broken out by a dimension and also needs a total, give the total and each breakdown member their own rule — a total that sums itself at the member level creates a circular reference. When a formula reads a value that lives outside its own dimension, pin the period explicitly so it resolves instead of returning zero. Guard any period-over-period difference so the opening balance is not counted as first-period activity. Make every assumption a named variable rather than a number buried in a formula, so it stays adjustable. |
| `anchored-simulator` | Use when building an interactive forecast tool, a what-if page, or a scenario explorer with controls over model variables. | Build the tool so that at default settings the simulated line sits exactly on the model baseline — every visible gap should be attributable to the driver the reader moved. Achieve that by re-running the model's own logic with the changed input, or by scaling the baseline by the ratio of the new setting to the default. Controls preview locally and never write to the model; save a setting only when the reader commits it, and always offer a reset back to baseline. Read cost rates and other assumptions from the model rather than hardcoding them, so the tool stays in sync. Before shipping, check that no two charts are the same curve rescaled — replace any duplicate with a ratio that actually moves, such as cost as a percent of revenue or profit per employee. |
| `public-company-calibration` | Use when modeling a real company from public information — a public filer, a franchisor with a disclosure document, or any business where reported figures should anchor the assumptions. | Before building anything, gather four to eight reported figures spanning volume, revenue, margin, and balance sheet, and record the period and source for each. Set the estimated inputs so the model reproduces those figures, then verify against a second, later figure to confirm the trajectory and not just the level. Save a note beside the model with three sections: figures that are reported, inputs that are estimated, and a side-by-side of model output against each reported figure. Add a fourth section for anything that does not tie, naming the driver that would close the gap. Never present a reconstruction as the company's actual results, and when the model disagrees with published guidance, say so and name the assumption rather than quietly retuning to match. |
| `headcount-from-capacity` | Use when building a hiring plan, a workforce forecast, a personnel cost model, or a department-level org plan. | Model headcount as a ratio to whatever the business physically scales on — stores, megawatts, accounts, shipments — rather than a compounding monthly hiring rate, which produces implausible totals over a multi-year horizon. Hold the ratio by department, let it drift on its own trend so an early hiring surge flattens into operating leverage later, and multiply by the driver to get headcount. Derive personnel cost from headcount, salary band, and a loaded-cost multiplier. Surface revenue per employee, units per employee, and support headcount per hundred units as checks on whether the plan stays sane. If the P&L also carries labor as a percent of revenue, tie the two together explicitly rather than leaving a bottom-up plan sitting beside a top-down rate with no reconciliation. |
